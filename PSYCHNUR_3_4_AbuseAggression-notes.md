# Abuse, Aggression, and Violence — PSYCHNUR 3.4

## Cram sheet

**Anger ≠ aggression ≠ violence:** anger = **feeling** · aggression = **intentional harmful behavior** · violence = **severe/extreme** aggression

**Early escalation cues:** pacing/restlessness · clenched fists, louder voice · **fixed staring** · threats · agitation, self-harm, property destruction · refusing food/fluids, trying to leave `[Engage]` — compare with **baseline**

**STAMP:** **S**taring · **T**one/volume · **A**nxiety · **M**umbling · **P**acing

**Order of response:** de-escalation first → least restrictive option that works (IM meds when PO/de-escalation fail) → restraint **last resort**

**Protect position:** not directly in front · outside striking distance · exit available (and don't make the client feel trapped) · call for help **early**

**B52 (IM):** diphenhydramine **50 mg** + haloperidol **5 mg** + lorazepam **2 mg** `[Engage]` · lowest effective dose

**Restraint/seclusion numbers `[Engage]`:** face-to-face eval within **1 hr** · monitor/document q**15 min** · RN assessment **hourly** · ROM q**2 hr** · **5** staff to apply (4 limbs + head) · upper extremities first · staff training **annually**

**Seclusion vs. time-out:** seclusion = **cannot** leave · time-out = client **chooses** and **can** leave

**Cycle of violence (Walker):** tension building → acute incident (hours up to **24 hr**) → honeymoon (apology, promises)

**Defense mechanisms:** **displacement** (emotion → safer target) · **undoing** (make up for the act)

**Interview survivors:** **alone**, away from suspected abuser · explain role · give choices · **ask before touch**

**Mandatory reporting:** children, older adults, vulnerable adults · report at the **legal threshold**, not personal certainty

**NSSH:** self-injury **without intent to die**, used to regulate emotion · one of the strongest predictors of **future suicide attempt** · never "attention-seeking"

**Active shooter:** **Run → Hide → Fight** (last resort) · mass-casualty care: **hemorrhage control**, airway, hypothermia prevention, triage

## I. Abuse: power and control

> [!quote] Anchor
> Abuse = power + control. It's usually a repeated pattern, not one event — and more than one form may be present.

- Abuse → ↑ risk for SUD, anxiety, depression, eating disorders, **BPD**, **PTSD** `[Engage]`. Keep assessment **trauma-informed**.

#### Forms of abuse and red flags?

| Form | What it is | Red flags |
|---|---|---|
| **Physical** | Force/injury (hitting, kicking, strangling, biting) | Bruises, burns, fractures, lacerations, punctures; **injuries in different stages of healing**; torn clothing, broken glasses |
| **Emotional** | Intimidation, threats, degradation | **Invisible on exam**; withdrawal, unusual emotional flatness, ↓ self-worth, **fear around a particular person** |
| **Sexual** | Unwanted contact, coercion, exploitation; online grooming | Sudden fear, behavior change, **regression** (bedwetting), sexual behavior beyond developmental level, genital/breast/anal injury, damaged/bloody underclothing |
| **Neglect/abandonment** | Basic needs unmet | Malnutrition, dehydration, poor hygiene, **untreated conditions, pressure injuries**, unsafe home |
| **Economic/financial** | Money or property used without consent | Unexplained withdrawals/transfers, **new names on accounts**, missing possessions or papers, client **doesn't remember signing** |

> [!tip] Instructor emphasis
> Findings **raise concern — they don't prove abuse**. Get the client **alone** and assess **without accusation**. Emotional abuse is the **hardest to identify** (no physical evidence). **Document the client's own words.**

> [!danger] Report immediately
> **Unexplained STI findings in a child** require investigation.

#### Think beyond one category?
- Combine physical clues + behavioral clues + the client's account. One type of abuse can point to another; keep the assessment open.

## II. Anger, aggression, violence: biology and risk

#### Biology of aggression?
- **Serotonin** regulates mood and impulse control; altered function → ↑ impulsivity/aggression.
- **Prefrontal cortex** → regulation; **limbic system/amygdala** → emotion and threat response. Injury/lesions here (TBI, tumor, Alzheimer's, temporal lobe epilepsy `[Engage]`) → aggression. Consider neurologic/medical contributors.
- Anger can be expressed safely and even drive positive change; unmanaged anger → HTN, headache, insomnia, GI issues `[Engage]`.

#### Who's at increased risk?
- Previous violence or **exposure** to violence · substance use · TBI/neurologic disease · **PTSD, bipolar I, ADHD, impulse-control disorders** · poor coping + low self-esteem · current feelings of **threat, frustration, powerlessness** (also feeling deceived, invalidated, treated unfairly `[Engage]`).
- `[Engage]` Also: no positive role models, recent major stressor (loss, divorce, unemployment within the past year), lower SES; older adults and women less likely.

> [!info] Constant predictors of violence `[Engage]`
> Inability to control aggressive impulses · not understanding consequences · lack of remorse · substance use. (Case: mother slams her fist on the table while yelling at her daughter = inability to control aggressive impulses.)

#### Why do ACEs matter?
- Adverse experiences **before age 18**: abuse, neglect, household violence, instability, caregiver substance misuse or untreated mental illness, family suicide `[Engage]`.
- Chronic (toxic) stress affects brain development → later health problems, conduct disorder, SUD; child may later become the aggressor.

#### Which defense mechanisms show up?

| Mechanism | What happens | Example `[Engage]` |
|---|---|---|
| **Displacement** | Emotion redirected to a **safer target** | Angry at coworker, yells at partner or child |
| **Undoing** | Tries to "make up for" unacceptable behavior | Degrades partner in public, buys them a gift the next day |

- Look at the **pattern**, not the isolated event.

## III. Abuse across the lifespan

#### Child abuse and neglect?
- **CAPTA**: any act or failure to act by a parent/caregiver causing death, serious physical/emotional harm, sexual abuse, exploitation — **or imminent risk of serious harm**.
- Neglect: food, shelter, clothing, supervision, medical care (most states include **failure to educate** `[Engage]`). State definitions vary → know the facility's **mandated-reporting pathway**.

#### Intimate partner violence?
- Physical/sexual violence, **stalking**, psychological aggression by a **current or former** partner; no sexual intimacy required.
- **Teen dating violence** is common (higher risk in LGBTQ+ teens `[Engage]`). Health effects persist for years (PTSD, depression, cardiac, GI, reproductive). **Financial dependence** makes leaving harder.

#### Cycle of violence phases?

| Phase | Features |
|---|---|
| **Tension building** | Criticism, control, fear; communication breaks down; can last days to years |
| **Acute incident** | Violence/explosion, rage; usually hours up to **24 hr**; police may be involved |
| **Honeymoon/reconciliation** | Apology, affection, promises to change — control continues through economic/emotional abuse |

- Repeated cycles → ↑ isolation and ↑ danger. Applies beyond IPV.

#### Older adult abuse?
- Dependence ↑ vulnerability; perpetrators often **family caregivers**.
- Watch for isolation, loss of decision-making. ==**Missed medications + financial control = concerning combination.**== Interview **privately**.

#### Rape, sexual assault, stalking, bullying?
- **Rape** = forced/coerced intercourse **with penetration**; **sexual assault** is broader (fondling, grabbing, threats — no penetration needed). Any gender. **Acquaintance** assaults are common (> **two-thirds** know the attacker `[Engage]`). **Statutory rape** = adult + person < **18**, even with "consent" `[Engage]`.
- Always reinforce: **responsibility belongs to the perpetrator.**
- **Stalking**: repeated unwanted attention/monitoring/threats; following, repeated calls/texts, showing up at home/work/school. **Safety planning is central.**
- **Bullying**: three core elements — **repeated**, **power imbalance**, **unwanted aggression**. Physical, verbal, social, property; cyberbullying extends it online. Prevention = education + assessment + support + coping skills.

> [!info] Bullying by prevention level `[Engage]`
> Primary: educate nurses, teachers, students, parents; protocols. Secondary: ask students about bullying, teach how to respond and cope. Tertiary: care for the bullied student; CBT, role-play, support groups.

## IV. Team approach, assessment, interviewing

#### What makes care safer?
- Client-centered plan · medical **and** mental status assessment · **consistent de-escalation language** across staff · reduce noise, crowding, triggers · share safety strategies across the team.
- `[Engage]` Unit strategies: posted behavior standards agreed on by clients and staff, sensory items for agitated clients.

#### Rule out medical causes — what workup?
- History + physical, **neurologic** assessment, psychiatric assessment.
- Possible labs: **CBC, thyroid, metabolic panel, urinalysis, drug screen** (chest x-ray `[Engage]`); more by presentation. Get behavior history from **more than one source** (e.g., a child's teacher) `[Engage]`.

#### How to interview a survivor?
- **Privacy first** — away from the person suspected of abuse. Everyone introduces themselves and explains their role.
- Slow down: **conversation, not interrogation**. Give choices (where to sit, when to break). **Ask permission before touch.**
- Trauma affects memory, concentration, emotion — multiple versions of a story are expected, not proof of lying.
- Survivors may deny, fear going home, distrust staff, neglect own needs, be sleep-deprived or malnourished `[Engage]`.

#### Trauma-informed vs. forensic interviewing?

| | Trauma-informed | Forensic |
|---|---|---|
| Purpose | Care and safety | **Investigative** — did a crime occur? |
| Core | Safety, choice, control, rapport; culture, language, gender | Often law enforcement + **SANE** collecting evidence |
| Goal | Engagement | Preserve evidence **while minimizing further trauma** |

#### Mandatory reporting?
- Vulnerable populations (children, older adults, cognitively/emotionally impaired) → nurses are **mandated reporters**.
- Follow **state law + organizational policy**. ==Reporting is based on required thresholds, not personal certainty.== Know exactly who to contact.

#### Checking your own bias?
- Notice automatic ("gut") reactions; separate the person from the behavior; replace assumptions with individualized assessment.
- **Pause and reset** (slow deep breaths) before entering a stressful interaction. Habit-breaking + replacing stereotypes + mindfulness `[Engage]`.

#### Therapeutic vs. non-therapeutic responses
Example phrasing built from lecture/textbook principles `[ADDED]`.

| Situation | Therapeutic | Non-therapeutic | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Partner won't leave during assessment | "I do a private part of every exam — I'll come get you when we're done." | Interviewing with the partner present | Suspected abuser must not be present |
| Survivor: "I shouldn't have gone to his place." | "What happened is not your fault. The person who hurt you is responsible." | "Why did you go there?" | Responsibility belongs to perpetrator; "why" blames |
| Agitated client yelling in hallway | "I can see you're upset. What would help right now?" (calm, low voice, space) | "Calm down or you're going to seclusion." | Acknowledge distress; threats and power struggles escalate |
| Client discloses cutting | "What was happening right before you cut? What does it do for you?" | "You're just doing this for attention." | Explore emotion/function; drop judgmental labels |
| Teen describes mother belittling her | "Tell me more about what she says to you." (document her words) | "I'm sure she doesn't mean it." | Take disclosures seriously; minimizing shuts them down |

## V. Managing aggression

#### How to assess aggression risk?
- Use structured tools when appropriate: **STAMP** (Staring, Tone/volume, Anxiety, Mumbling, Pacing), **Brøset Violence Checklist**; also OAS-M, BRACHA (children/adolescents) `[Engage]`. Match tool to setting; **reassess** as condition changes.
- Aggression often signals **distress or an unmet need** `[Engage]`.

> [!warning] Priority: de-escalation comes first
> Reduce stimulation / remove the trigger (other client, staff) · give space · **calm, controlled voice** · acknowledge distress · **ask what would help** · avoid arguing, threats, power struggles. Showing anger escalates.

#### How does the nurse protect her position?
- Don't stand directly in front; stay **outside striking distance** (arm's length). Keep an **exit available** — and don't block the doorway so the client feels trapped `[Engage]`. Know the room before escalation. **Call for help early.**

#### Medications for aggression?
- Choice depends on **cause and severity**. Classes: antipsychotics, benzodiazepines, mood stabilizers, anticonvulsants, antidepressants.
- **IM** when oral meds and de-escalation fail. Use the **lowest effective dose**; monitor response, adverse effects, interactions.

| Drug (B52 `[Engage]`) | Class / role | Monitor `[ADDED]` |
|---|---|---|
| **Haloperidol 5 mg** | First-gen antipsychotic; calms agitation/psychosis | EPS/acute dystonia, **QT prolongation**, NMS (fever, rigidity) |
| **Lorazepam 2 mg** | Benzodiazepine; sedation | **Respiratory depression**, hypotension, oversedation |
| **Diphenhydramine 50 mg** | Antihistamine/anticholinergic; sedation, prevents dystonia | Anticholinergic effects (dry mouth, retention, confusion) |

- Others `[Engage]`: olanzapine, ziprasidone, droperidol, chlorpromazine, midazolam, diazepam, promethazine.
- A med given to control behavior or restrict movement = **chemical restraint** (e.g., haloperidol, midazolam, diazepam) `[Engage]`.

#### What else prevents aggression on the unit?
- **Boredom ↑ aggression and self-harm** `[Engage]` → physical activity (walks with staff), music, community groups.
- **Contingency management**: reward desired behavior (staying calm, no property destruction) with small concrete rewards (even a handwritten note) `[Engage]`.

## VI. Seclusion and restraint

> [!quote] Anchor
> Least restrictive option first. Never for punishment, coercion, or staff convenience.

#### Seclusion vs. time-out?

| | Seclusion | Time-out |
|---|---|---|
| Who decides | Staff | **Client chooses** ("I need a break") |
| Can leave? | **No** — physically prevented | **Yes**, whenever they choose |
| Use | Serious safety situations only (violent/self-injurious) | Any time as a coping tool |

#### When can restraints be used?
- **Last resort** when necessary for safety (danger to self/others) after less restrictive measures fail. Follow **policy, law, and an authorized order**.
- Order must include reason, type, duration, and **release criteria** `[Engage]`. May be initiated in an emergency by qualified staff, followed by a provider prescription `[Engage]`.
- `[ADDED — verify vs course materials]` Orders are time-limited (CMS: **4 hr** adults, **2 hr** ages 9–17, **1 hr** under 9) and **never PRN**.
- Application `[Engage]`: **5** staff (one per limb + head); **upper extremities first**; supine, one arm above the head and one at the side; check that restraints don't impair circulation or cause pain.

> [!danger] While restrained or secluded
> Never leave a restrained client alone. Face-to-face evaluation by provider/RN/PA within **1 hr** · observe and document q**15 min** (injury, breathing, circulation) · RN assessment **hourly** (VS incl. pulse ox, circulation, skin, mental status, offer fluids) · ROM q**2 hr** · hydration, toileting, hygiene, nutrition · protect dignity and privacy · encourage behaviors that lead to release. `[Engage]`

#### When do restrictions end?
- When the client meets the **established release criteria**. Reassess **sooner** if risk changes or a medical concern arises.
- Document: triggers, de-escalation attempts and response, intervention used, monitoring findings, comfort measures, response to release.

#### Debriefing — who and what?
- **Staff debrief** (timely, facilitator-led): review facts, share thoughts and reactions, identify triggers and what worked, coping/support needs, lessons learned; end on a positive note.
- **Client debrief** after every seclusion/restraint, **as soon as possible after release** `[Engage]`: listen to the client's view, correct misperceptions, recognize any trauma, support return to milieu, plan different approaches, guide them if they believe rights were violated. Include family only **with client permission**.

#### Clinical judgment for aggression?
- Recognize cues → analyze → **prioritize safety hypotheses** → choose interventions (meds, de-escalation, seclusion/restraint) → act + monitor → evaluate and revise.

## VII. Nonsuicidal self-harm (NSSH)

#### What is NSSH?
- **Intentional self-injury without stated intent to die** at that moment. Cutting most common; also burning, scratching, biting, carving, hair pulling, head banging.
- Function: manage overwhelming emotion ("to feel something"). Common with **BPD, substance use, eating disorders**. Often begins in adolescence (appearance dissatisfaction, school pressure; poverty, abuse/neglect, family problems `[Engage]`).

> [!info] Key contrast: NSSH vs. factitious disorder `[Engage]`
> NSSH clients rarely **seek** medical attention. Factitious disorder = self-injury or faked symptoms **in order to get** medical attention.

#### Warning signs?
- Unexplained scars or fresh injuries · **hidden sharp objects** · **covering arms/legs despite weather** · impulsive emotional changes · worthlessness, helplessness, hopelessness · poor relationships, identity issues `[Engage]`.

> [!danger] Never minimize
> NSSH is one of the most dependable predictors of a **future suicide attempt**. Assess suicide risk. Complications: infection, scarring, bleeding, shame, isolation `[Engage]`.

#### Nursing response?
- Drop judgmental labels (not "manipulative" or "attention-seeking").
- Ask **what happened before** the behavior; explore the **emotion/function** ("What do you feel when you do this?").
- Connect to treatment and support; build alternative coping (reach out to a trusted person, drawing/journaling, exercise, refocusing); ==create a plan for future urges==; educate family.

> [!example] Worked example — Margaret, 15 `[Engage]`
> Admitted after a fluoxetine overdose. Withdrawn around her mother, who yells and slams the table during a visit. In a private session she discloses belittling and 6 months of cutting that "no one noticed." Nursing moves: interview **alone** and let her control breaks; document her words; assess for emotional abuse (mandatory report threshold); assess NSSH and suicide risk; team plan = antidepressant, CBT, family therapy, community resources.

## VIII. Community violence and forensic nursing

#### Nurse's role in community violence?
- Violence can occur in any setting; preparedness protects patients **and** staff. Know the facility/community emergency plan.
- Care priorities: ==**hemorrhage control**==, airway, **hypothermia prevention**, triage/transfer.

#### Run, Hide, Fight?
- **Run**: escape if possible; call **911** when safe. **Hide**: secure/lock the area if you can't escape. **Fight**: absolute **last resort**.
- Drills speed decisions. You must survive to help others.

#### Forensic nursing and SANE?
- Forensic nurse: RN/APRN with added education in violence and victimization — sexual assault, IPV, child/older-adult abuse, death investigation, corrections, disasters.
- **SANE** = sexual assault nurse examiner: focused exam, **evidence collection** and preservation, meds to treat/prevent STIs, documentation, **expert testimony**.

## Self-quiz

> [!question]- 1. Which form of abuse is hardest for nurses to identify, and why?
> Emotional — no physical evidence.

> [!question]- 2. An older adult has missed medications and her son now controls her bank account. What's the concern and next step?
> Neglect plus financial exploitation; interview her privately and follow the mandatory-reporting pathway.

> [!question]- 3. Anger, aggression, violence: define each in one word or phrase.
> Anger = feeling; aggression = intentional harmful behavior; violence = severe/extreme aggression.

> [!question]- 4. Which warning signs typically precede violence: ripping pages from a book, refusing meals, asking for the rights advocate, pacing, trying to leave before discharge, fleeting eye contact?
> Ripping pages (destroying objects), refusing meals, pacing, trying to leave before discharge.

> [!question]- 5. Angry at his boss, a client screams at his wife. Defense mechanism?
> Displacement.

> [!question]- 6. After humiliating his partner at a party, a man buys her flowers. Defense mechanism, and which cycle phase does it resemble?
> Undoing; honeymoon/reconciliation phase.

> [!question]- 7. List the three phases of the cycle of violence.
> Tension building → acute incident → honeymoon/reconciliation.

> [!question]- 8. A woman arrives with her husband, who answers every question. First action?
> Interview her alone, away from the husband.

> [!question]- 9. During a survivor interview, which action is therapeutic: bring in the parent, assign a seat, ask when she'd like a break, or touch her shoulder first?
> Ask when she would like a break (gives control).

> [!question]- 10. The nurse suspects, but isn't certain of, child abuse. Report?
> Yes — reporting is based on the legal threshold (suspicion), not certainty.

> [!question]- 11. A client is pacing and yelling. First intervention?
> De-escalation: reduce stimulation, give space, calm voice, acknowledge distress, ask what would help.

> [!question]- 12. Where should the nurse stand with an escalating client?
> Not directly in front, outside striking distance, with an exit available and without making the client feel trapped.

> [!question]- 13. Components and doses of "B52"?
> Haloperidol 5 mg + lorazepam 2 mg + diphenhydramine 50 mg IM.

> [!question]- 14. A client says "I need a break" and goes to a quiet room with the door open. Seclusion?
> No — time-out (client chose it and can leave).

> [!question]- 15. How soon is a face-to-face evaluation required after restraint, and how often is the client monitored?
> Within 1 hr; monitor and document every 15 min (RN assessment hourly, ROM every 2 hr).

> [!question]- 16. Staff want to keep a client in restraints overnight "so he doesn't bother anyone." Acceptable?
> No — restraints are never for convenience or punishment; release when criteria are met.

> [!question]- 17. When is the client debrief after restraint done?
> As soon as possible after release — every time.

> [!question]- 18. A teen hides razors and wears long sleeves in summer. What is suspected and what else must be assessed?
> NSSH; assess suicide risk — NSSH strongly predicts future suicide attempts.

> [!question]- 19. Best question for a client who just self-injured?
> "What happened right before you did this?" / "What do you feel when you do this?"

> [!question]- 20. Which risk factor places a 14-year-old at high risk for NSSH: poverty, missing a favorite sport, family history of diabetes, no weekday video games?
> History of poverty.

> [!question]- 21. Active shooter, exit is clear. What do you do?
> Run — escape, then call 911 when safe.

> [!question]- 22. Top care priority for mass-casualty victims?
> Hemorrhage control (then airway, hypothermia prevention, triage).

> [!question]- 23. Rape vs. sexual assault?
> Rape involves forced/coerced penetration; sexual assault is broader and needs no penetration.
