## I. Terminology and trends

> [!quote] Anchor
> About 1 in 5 children has a special health care need. Technology keeps more of them alive and at home, so families now carry much of the medical care themselves.

#### Who has a "special health care need"?
- A child who has, or is at risk for, a chronic **physical, developmental, emotional, or behavioral** condition requiring more care than children generally.
- **19.4%** of U.S. children **< 17 yr**; the rate rises as care and technology advance. Instructor emphasis: ==the burden on child and family==.
- Behavioral and mental health disorders (e.g., depression) often co-occur with physical conditions. About **30%** of U.S. children and adolescents have a psychiatric diagnosis, and rates are rising `[Engage: Trends]`.

#### Chronic illness vs. disability?
| | Chronic illness | Disability |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Disease lasting **> 12 months**; requires continued medical care; impacts daily life | Physical or mental condition that makes activities or interaction with the environment harder |
| Impact depends on | Disease control | Degree of impairment, activity limitation, restricted participation |
| Examples | ==**Asthma**: most common; #1 cause of school absence==; diabetes, food allergies, epilepsy; depression, ADHD, congenital heart disease `[Engage]` | Hearing loss, vision loss, **cerebral palsy**; communication, intellectual, or learning impairments; trouble with social relationships `[Engage]` |

- **Both:** lupus, vision loss. **Illness only:** asthma. Assess each condition alone and together for functional impact `[Engage]`.
- **Cerebral palsy** `[ADDED]`: non-progressive motor disorder from brain injury before, during, or soon after birth. Watch for contractures, aspiration, and seizures. It's the module's model physical disability (case study, IV).

#### How has technology changed care?
- Devices like **LVADs** (left ventricular assist devices) → longer survival but more complex needs.
- Care has shifted home → ==parents perform medical tasks== → more stress, responsibility, financial and emotional strain.

#### Who is at risk for disability? `[Engage]`
- Prenatal alcohol or substance exposure; **lead** exposure; poverty/low SES; parent with a mental health disorder; racism, neglect, toxic stress; foster care; family history of mental health or neurodevelopmental disorders.
- High-risk infants: **preterm**, neonatal asphyxia, neonatal infection, significant hyperbilirubinemia, advanced resuscitation.

## II. Development across the lifespan

> [!quote] Anchor
> Chronic illness threatens each stage's developmental task. Screen for delay early, support the stressed caregiver, and protect what the stage needs: attachment, autonomy, school, independence.

### Infant (< 1 yr)

#### Why is infancy the critical window?
- First ==**1,000 days**== drive brain development; chronic illness or disability → risk of **developmental delay**. Developmental surveillance and early intervention are imperative.
- **Early childhood intervention (ECI)** starts as soon as a disability is identified; maximizes function and lifelong development; delivered in daycare centers, outpatient centers, or the home `[Engage]`.
- Tool: **Bayley Scales of Infant and Toddler Development** (BSID-II / Bayley-III). [NOTE: current practice: the current edition is Bayley-4.]

#### What to screen at every visit?
- ==Language, motor, visual abilities, communication patterns.==
- Possible early **ASD** signs: ==not recognizing own name, not pointing at objects== `[Engage]`. Also feeding difficulty, delayed walking or speech.
- Speech delay → refer to **hearing specialist** and speech therapy `[Engage]`.

#### Neuroprotective care?
- **Skin-to-skin (kangaroo care):** ↓ stress biomarkers in infant and mother, ↓ preterm mortality; continued use → less mechanical ventilation, shorter NICU stay.
- ==Limit painful procedures to necessary ones.== Pain *and* its treatment affect brain development; early-childhood **opioids** are linked to cerebellar injury and poor neurodevelopmental outcomes. During procedures: skin-to-skin or maternal breast milk.
- **Breast milk** (donor if unavailable) → brain health, less growth failure. Time care around sleep cycles. Encourage parents in the NICU `[Engage]`.
- At home: reading, stimulating play, free movement. Engaged parenting → better cognitive/behavioral outcomes; negative parenting → externalizing behaviors `[Engage]`.

### Toddler (1–3 yr)

#### When are standardized assessments due?
- **12 and 18 mo:** fine motor, language, previously missed motor delays. **30 mo:** motor, language, cognitive → need for early intervention. Done in the medical home.
- [NOTE: current practice: AAP recommends developmental screening at **9, 18, and 30 mo** plus autism-specific screening at **18 and 24 mo**. The Engage summary also lists 9, 18, 30 mo and 4 yr.]
- Parent resources: CDC "Learn the Signs. Act Early."; AAP Screening Technical Assistance and Resource Center `[Engage]`.

#### Why is this stage hard on parents?
- Toddlers can't be reasoned with about ==pulling at tubes and equipment== (feeding tubes, oxygen) → added stress. Parents report poor sleep and overprotectiveness `[Engage]`.
- Nurse: ask about coping, rest, and emotional needs; encourage **meditation, mindfulness, support groups, counseling, self-care**. Targeted caregiving education (e.g., for congenital heart disease) ↓ parental stress `[Engage]`.
- Organized, quiet homes with consistent routines → fewer behavior problems in ASD/ADHD `[Engage]`.

#### What is augmented-language intervention (ALI)?
- For children with disabilities who have trouble communicating. Speech therapist sessions **2×/wk for 12 wk**; parents get home exercises.
- Method `[ADDED — verify]`: the adult models words on a speech-generating device or symbol board during everyday talk. Nurse role: refer, and reinforce home practice.

#### How to protect toddler autonomy?
- ==Offer limited choices== (two shirts, two snacks), even with complex medical needs. Parents may resist → teach the developmental tasks of each age.
- Monitor **height, weight, nutrition**; balanced diet and active play `[Engage]`.

#### Who does what on the team?
| Role | Contribution |
|---|---|
| **Pediatrician** | Diagnoses and manages; monitors health; coordinates care |
| **Physical therapist** | Mobility, strength, motor milestones, ↓ pain |
| **Occupational therapist** | Fine motor; adapts ADLs, self-care, play |
| **Social worker** | Psychosocial support; resources; system navigation |

- Example condition in the slide: juvenile rheumatoid arthritis. [NOTE: current practice: now called juvenile idiopathic arthritis (JIA).] Regular team meetings → coordinated plans, better quality of life.

### Preschool (3–6 yr)

#### Who needs screening here?
- Preschoolers are ==highly dependent on caregivers== for daily care and meds → ==screen caregivers for depression and stress==; refer. These parents have more stress and depression than parents of healthy children.
- Children don't understand the illness but can act in ways that affect it: a child with diabetes sneaks high-carb food `[Engage]`.
- Uncooperativeness and avoidance can be developmentally normal but add daily stress (child behavior and parent stress feed each other) `[Engage]`.

#### School readiness and delays?
- Assess **4–5-yr-olds** closely for ==school readiness and milestones==. Without screening, **over half** of developmental problems go undiagnosed.
- Support realistic milestones (e.g., **potty training**).
- Most common: ==speech/language delays== and developmental disabilities → **speech therapy**.
- ==Inclusive preschool==: children with mild disabilities gain more language and social skills than in special-ed classes, with no harm to typically developing peers.
- Not all states include chronic health conditions in early-intervention eligibility; assess developmental, behavioral, and medical needs together `[Engage]`.

#### What helps families adapt?
- ==Family cohesion== → better adaptation, acceptance of limits, routines, and adherence. Acceptance = finding meaning in the illness `[Engage]`.

### School age (6–12 yr)

#### How does chronic illness affect school?
- Affects academics, attendance, peer interaction, psychological health.
- ==**> 2×** as likely to be absent==; some miss as often as every **2 wk**. Top causes: **neurodevelopmental conditions, migraines**. Absenteeism → truancy, poor school completion, adult unemployment `[Engage]`.
- **Learning disabilities** are often diagnosed now → less postsecondary education, more adult mental illness and employment problems. **Intellectual disability** → impaired social functioning → loneliness, mental illness `[Engage]`.

#### School interventions?
- Personalized **attendance plan** with the school; schedule ==well visits outside school hours== `[Engage]`.
- Advocate for **assistive technology, individualized education plans, remote assignments, home-bound instruction** during exacerbations.
- **IEP** `[ADDED]`: written plan required by law (IDEA) for specialized instruction and services when a disability affects learning. Nurse supplies health information and advocates.

> [!todo] Not covered
> 504 plans (often contrasted with IEPs) aren't in either source.

#### Coping and mental health supports?
| Support | What it is |
|---|---|
| **Psychoeducation** | Teaching child and family about the illness, its treatment, and coping `[ADDED]` |
| **CBT**, individual counseling, peer support groups | Recommended for coping |
| **Biofeedback** | Child learns to control body signals (heart rate, muscle tension) shown on a monitor; used for pain/migraine `[ADDED]` |
| **Motivational interviewing** | Collaborative talk that draws out the child's own reasons to change; supports adherence `[ADDED]` |
| **Art, music, dance therapy** | Help children cope |

- Encourage socialization; assess psychosocial needs at every visit `[Engage]`.

#### Challenges in mainstream school?
- Isolation, low self-esteem, trouble forming friendships. Help: inclusive classrooms, **peer support programs**, individualized support plans, educating peers about disabilities.
- Children with disabilities join fewer structured (clubs) and unstructured (recess) activities; self-esteem and perceived classroom role predict participation. Caregivers who join in model confidence `[Engage]`.
- Peers with similar conditions offer hope, shared learning, emotional support `[Engage]`.

### Adolescent (12–20 yr)

#### Why does adherence drop?
- Seeking autonomy → transition to self-management → ==↓ adherence, worse short- and long-term outcomes==. Drivers: competing priorities, fitting in, rebellion, skipping time-consuming treatments, fatalism about lifespan `[Engage]`.
- ==Assess executive functioning== before handing over self-management. **Executive functioning** `[ADDED]`: planning, organizing, working memory, impulse control — what remembering doses, refills, and appointments depends on.
- ASD/ADHD → more family and social stress (social and executive-function deficits).
- High risk for poor psychological outcomes: **invisible disabilities** (e.g., chronic fatigue syndrome), chronic pain, uncertain prognosis `[Engage]`.

#### Psychosocial risks?
- More poor body image, low self-esteem, conflict with parents, interpersonal difficulties, lack of independence.
- Maladaptive behaviors raise risk of obesity, **self-harm, depression, suicide** → ==early behavioral health referral== `[Engage]`. Ask about suicidal thoughts directly; asking doesn't increase risk `[ADDED]`.
- ==Screen for risky behaviors at the same rate as healthy peers== (they report less substance use). Teens who see their illness as serious adopt healthier behaviors `[Engage]`.

#### Sexual health with a disability?

> [!warning] Priority
> Teens with disabilities have **3×** the risk of sexual violence, highest at **15–17 yr**. They get inadequate reproductive and contraception counseling → more STIs and unplanned pregnancies, and fewer breast/cervical screenings.

- Sex education is skipped on the false belief they have fewer sexual feelings. Teach: puberty changes, gender identity, sexual feelings, healthy relationships, ==consent, inappropriate touching and how to report it==, online safety. ==Match wording to cognitive level.== `[Engage]`
- Teens with any chronic illness know less about contraception → educate.
- Confidentiality, autonomy, communication preferences, and an ==accessible clinic== (e.g., wheelchair-to-exam-table transfer) as teens start seeking care alone `[Engage]`.

#### Menstruation with a disability? `[Engage]`
- Difficulty with pads, tampons, hygiene. **Menstrual suppression**: ==not before the first period==; include the teen in choosing (pill, IUD, patch, injection).
- Most choose oral contraceptives, levonorgestrel IUD, or patch; continuous hormones often → amenorrhea.
- **Hysterectomy** only for cancer or other medical need once all else fails; it does not prevent mood changes.

> [!danger] Red flag
> Hormonal contraception ↑ **thrombus** risk, especially with immobility from disability or chronic illness. `[Engage]`

#### Balancing parent and teen roles?
- Responsibility shifts gradually from **10 to 17 yr**. Helpful: family check-ins and reminders; parents handle supportive tasks (refills, appointments) and step in when the teen is overwhelmed `[Engage]`.
- Parents may swing between overprotection and conflict. Nurse teaches parents to support autonomy, teaches the teen self-management, and opens family communication.
- ==Talk to the teen directly==: adolescents feel invisible when providers address only parents; they value honesty and consistent providers `[Engage]`.

## III. Supporting the child and family

> [!quote] Anchor
> The family delivers most of the care. Nursing support works through the family: their stress, knowledge, resources, and coordination.

### Vulnerable populations

#### Who is vulnerable, and why does it matter?
- ↑ morbidity and mortality from lack of resources and inequities, worse with complex conditions and disability. Assess vulnerable status when assessing risk; advocate.
- Low income; minority groups (==Latino and African American children use emergency services more==); LGBTQIA+; chronic health conditions.
- Intellectual disability barriers `[Engage]`: inaccessible health information, low health literacy, cost, transportation, untrained providers, exclusion from decisions and consent, communication impairment, distrust after discrimination, stigma.

### Therapeutic relationships

#### How is a therapeutic relationship built?
- Starts at the **first interaction**; trust depends on listening skills and body language.
- ==Use the child's and caregiver's names== (→ better adherence `[Engage]`); active listening; open body language.
- **Set boundaries**; stay objective about the child's priority needs; stay client-focused; ==don't share personal information==.
- Barriers `[Engage]`: short staffing, task-oriented care, loud/chaotic setting, lack of privacy, abusive client or parent, provider dismissing feelings.

#### How does culture affect care?
- Shapes communication, access to early intervention, routines. Barriers: stigma, prejudice, provider knowledge gaps; extended-family pressure to avoid stigma can delay diagnosis `[Engage]`.
- Some cultures may see illness-related behavior as parental failure or hesitate to promote the child's independence. Immigrant families with language barriers → children miss more school. Use ==cultural humility== and translators `[Engage]`.

#### What do siblings experience?
- Feel **invisible**, ==guilty for being healthy==, jealous, insecure; absorb caregiving and financial stress; hide needs to be the "good child" → anxiety, depression. Positives: maturity, pride `[Engage]`.
- **Parentification** `[ADDED]`: a child takes on adult caregiving or emotional roles, which harms their own development.
- Highest risk: ==**4–11 yr**==. Help: social support, **sibling support groups** (↑ self-esteem, ↓ isolation), age-appropriate education about the condition, **child life** referral. Few services exist `[Engage]`.
- PICU/inpatient visits: ==usually better to visit with support==; child life prepares the sibling and debriefs through therapeutic play `[Engage]`.

### Emotional impact and coping

#### What does the family go through?
- Caregivers manage everything in a **fragmented system** and navigate specialty services alone (assess **health literacy**).
- Diagnosis → **uncertainty, grief** ("grieving the ideal child" `[Engage]`), adjusted expectations. ==Highest at diagnosis; returns with new challenges and transitions.==
- Parents may be frustrated after being told to "wait and see" about delays `[Engage]`.
- At diagnosis: crisis → family-centered counseling referral. In school-age children, mental illness often first appears at physical-illness diagnosis → discuss mental health early `[Engage]`.

#### What stresses parents? `[Engage]`
- Work plus caregiving; torn between parent and caretaker roles; doing painful procedures (e.g., NG insertion); ==sleep deprivation== (relationship strain, near-miss errors); guilt about siblings; outings need equipment; housing needs space for equipment.
- Marital strain, disagreement on severity, single parents (usually mothers) overwhelmed, stepfamily friction.
- Stress scales with perceived severity, treatment burden, and threat to life. Parents of children with developmental disability are more often depressed.

#### Child mental health: who's at risk?
- Chronic physical illness → more comorbid mental health disorders (QOL, illness burden, bullying, family strain, neuro effects). Highest: **neurological disorders, migraines, atopic disorders** (asthma, eczema) `[Engage]`.
- ==Screen with a validated tool soon after diagnosis and at every visit==; encourage school attendance `[Engage]`.

#### Adaptive vs. avoidant coping?
| | Adaptive | Avoidant |
|---|---|---|
| Looks like | Problem-based coping (find the stress source), stress management, acceptance, support network | Disengagement; not thinking about feelings (**psychological inflexibility**) |
| Outcome | ↓ distress, ↓ burden, better QOL | Brief relief → anxiety, depression, stress |

- Parental burnout and anxiety → worse child adjustment. Social support and life satisfaction build parental **resilience** `[Engage]`.

### NAPC model

#### How does NAPC open a mental health conversation?

> [!example] Mnemonic — NAPC
> **N**ormalize: caring for a child with chronic illness is hard for most families
> **A**sk: a general question about how they're doing
> **P**ause: listen and reflect, ==no charting, no distractions==
> **C**onnect: restate what was said; offer resources or referrals

- Why: caregiving ↑ distress, anxiety, mood, traumatic stress, behavior disorders; ==parental mental health strongly predicts child outcomes==.
- ==Ensure privacy==: children may not speak freely with parents present, and parents may not with the child present → talk separately. Chart afterward; share mental health info with the team. Start at diagnosis and repeat every visit; no special training needed `[Engage]`.

> [!info] Key contrast
> Parent: "I haven't slept a full night since the diagnosis."
> **Therapeutic:** "Many parents find this exhausting. How are you holding up?" (normalize, open question) `[ADDED]`
> **Non-therapeutic:** "At least she's stable now." (minimizes) · "You need to take better care of yourself." (lecturing) `[ADDED]`

### Caregiver supports

#### What reduces caregiver stress?
- **Respite care**: short break from caregiving (hours to weeks, in or out of home); also widens the child's social world. Hard to find: ==families of children with behavioral problems need it most and find it hardest==; cost is a barrier `[Engage]`.
- **Financial assistance** (treatment, equipment, time off work).
- **Peer support**: group of caregivers with similar-age children.
- **Psychological therapy**: offer in-person and virtual. **Acceptance and commitment therapy (ACT)** builds on CBT; evidence-based for parents. ACT `[ADDED]`: accept painful thoughts and feelings instead of avoiding them, then commit to values-based actions (the counter to avoidant coping).
- Nurse: ask parents about *their* needs; teach against avoidance; mindfulness; web-based therapy or guided self-help `[Engage]`.
- **LTSS** (long-term services and supports): attendant care, private duty nursing, assistive technology, non-medical transport, case management `[Engage]`.

### Complex medical needs: ADLs and transport

#### What ADL support is needed?
- Feeding, bathing, ambulating/position changes, grooming, dressing, bowel/bladder control, toileting.

#### Safe transport rules?
- ==All children ride in child safety seats==, whatever their condition; follow law and manufacturer limits (weight, height, rear vs. forward) `[Engage]`.
- **Poor trunk control:** belt-positioning or standard booster for trunk support; lap vests, specialized medical seats, extra tethers.
- ==Refer to a **CPST**== (certified child passenger safety technician) for special-needs restraints.
- **Prone** positioning → car bed. Severe scoliosis → large medical seat or adaptive restraints.
- **Wheelchair** `[Engage]`: transit-option wheelchair is the chair of choice; if the child rides in it: certified transit model, ==**4-point tie-down**==, lap tray removed and secured separately.

> [!warning] Priority
> **Trach/complex airway:** seat angle may need adjusting; ==a trained passenger who can assess the airway and manage dislodgement== rides along. Carry a spare trach (same size + one smaller), obturator, suction, and bag-valve mask `[ADDED — verify]`.
> **Equipment** (oxygen, monitors, wheelchairs) becomes a projectile → secure ==below the window line== (under the seat or in the trunk).
> **Electric equipment:** ==twice the power + fully charged backup + emergency vehicle connection==; check with the equipment provider.

### Primary care and coordination

#### What is a medical home?
- `[ADDED]`: a primary-care practice that serves as the child's home base. Care is accessible, continuous, comprehensive, family-centered, coordinated, compassionate, and culturally effective. It links specialists and community services. Children with complex illness need one `[Engage summary]`.
- Consistent provider → continuity and trust. Specialists and PCP communicate openly; interdisciplinary team includes a **mental health specialist** and **translators** `[Engage]`. Coordinated specialty networks are often tied to children's hospitals; the nurse is often central.
- ==Timely primary care + early recognition → early intervention → better outcomes.==

#### Family-centered care and shared decisions?
- ==Parents are the primary caregivers== → involve them in decisions. Care should be accessible, culturally effective, evidence-based, coordinated, equitable, child- and family-centered `[Engage]`.
- Parenting difficulties (poor mental health, conflict, child behavior problems) → poor adherence. Affectionate, consistent, responsive parenting → better adherence and outcomes `[Engage]`.
- **Shared decision-making** → more satisfaction and adherence and stronger relationships, but takes more time and education resources. ==Assess the child's cognitive, psychological, and physiological capacity and interest.== Present all options, including not treating `[Engage]`.

#### What does care coordination achieve?
- ↑ access, ↑ family satisfaction, ==↓ ED visits==. Team: medical home, schools, insurers, community agencies. Emotional/behavioral/developmental needs need more coordination than physical needs alone `[Engage]`.
- School coordination: treatments needed at school, education needs. Barriers: HIPAA concerns, provider time, no billing. Only **25%** of parents report adequate coordination `[Engage]`.

#### Insurance?
- Most children: parent's employer plan; some employers offer none or **high-deductible** plans (large out-of-pocket costs before coverage starts).
- **Medicaid/CHIP:** nearly **half** of children with special health care needs; lower SES, historically marginalized groups, higher needs. ==Limited cost-sharing== → more affordable; higher satisfaction and specialist/behavioral access `[Engage]`.
- **CHIP** `[ADDED]`: covers children whose families earn too much for Medicaid but can't afford private coverage. Uninsured families → social work referral.

#### Caregiver education?
- Home skills (trach care, suctioning, **parenteral feedings**) → ↓ exacerbations, complications, ==readmissions==. Parents often report inadequate teaching → stress.
- **Parenteral feedings** `[ADDED]`: IV nutrition through a central line; teaching centers on aseptic line care and infection signs.
- ==First assess ability to learn, learning preferences, needs, and health literacy.== Use visuals, videos, written materials, demonstrations, **teach-back**. Good teaching ↑ self-efficacy.
- **Teach-back** `[ADDED]`: caregiver explains or demonstrates in their own words; the nurse re-teaches gaps. "Do you understand?" is not evaluation.
- Asthma example `[Engage]`: prescriptions ==filled before discharge== → fewer readmissions. One-on-one teaching (action plan, inhaler demo, adherence barriers, certified asthma educator, teach-back) → better control.

### Bullying, violence, maltreatment

#### Who is at risk, and why?
- ==Higher parental stress → ↑ abuse, neglect, maltreatment== of children with special health care needs → closely screen for non-accidental trauma.
- **Non-accidental trauma** `[ADDED]`: injury that doesn't fit the history or the child's development, delayed care-seeking, patterned bruises/burns, bruising on a non-mobile infant. Reasonable suspicion is enough to report.
- **Cognitive disabilities** (low intellectual function, poor executive function, impulsivity): seen as **defiant or lazy**; disability isn't apparent → ==more bullying and maltreatment==. Visible physical disabilities (wheelchair) → less ridicule and bullying.
- More adverse childhood events → acting out in adolescence; higher in **intellectual** than physical disability. Help: **peer support groups, life skills training**.

#### Externalizing vs. internalizing?
| | Externalizing | Internalizing `[ADDED]` |
|---|---|---|
| Direction | Outward | Inward |
| Looks like | Hyperactive, aggressive, disruptive (~**half** of children with special health care needs) | Anxiety, depression, withdrawal, somatic complaints |
| Effect | Family dysfunction, worse health outcomes | Easily missed; screen |

> [!danger] Report immediately
> Suspected abuse, neglect, or bullying-related harm → report to the appropriate authorities. ==The duty to protect outweighs confidentiality==; protect privacy in everything else.

### Play

#### Why and how to use play?
- Play is a universal right and essential to development; family play → engagement and stress coping.
- ==Play within the restrictions of the disease.==
- In the hospital: distraction and coping; **child life** for the child *and* siblings; play therapy referral; age-appropriate toys, TV, electronics.
- Tech (VR, apps): engagement, personalization, distraction from pain vs. less face-to-face play, cost, training, screen time, unequal access.

### Hospitalization

#### How to support a hospitalized child with a disability?
- Family-centered care; ==24/7 parental presence== → ↓ anxiety, better coping.
- Longer lengths of stay; providers report discomfort and knowledge gaps with disabilities `[Engage]`. Encourage **adaptive coping** (→ better QOL).
- **SSNR** (safe, supportive, nurturing relationships) build resilience. **Toxic stress** `[Engage]` = the body's biological response to adversity (bullying, inequality, isolation). Positive experiences (shared reading, responsive caregivers, play) buffer it.
- Providers: screen for **maternal depression**, link to food/nutrition programs, teach appraisal, executive function, self-care, and positive parenting.
- **Empower:** ==extra time to complete tasks; resist doing it for them==. Include parents in hospital care (recognizes their expertise) `[Engage]`.
- **Goals:** progress is slower → ==measurable, achievable short- and long-term goals==; celebrate progress; display goals visibly in the room (deck) or in writing/digitally (Engage).
- **Communication:** scheduled family meetings (a few times a week) → discharge planning, teaching needs; consistent providers or a primary intensivist; ==more frequent rounding==.

#### Long-term goals? `[Engage]`
- ==Prevent readmission==: thorough family training; partnerships between acute-care and home-care nursing.
- **Integrative care**: families get resources to seek care early → fewer ED visits and asthma exacerbations, better follow-up and adherence, lower cost.
- **WHO ICF framework**: shifts from treating manifestations to ==function and participation==; the child/family sets goals using the **F-words**: function, family, fun, friends, future (original model also includes **fitness** `[ADDED — verify]`).
- Goal-setting: measurable, achievable, incremental, built on the child's interests (family sets them if child too young). Keep it engaging and developmentally appropriate; ==stop if it causes pain and comfort the child==; practice at home/community; parents coach. Adaptive equipment and assistive tech for leisure with peers.

## IV. Nursing process (clinical judgment)

#### Six steps in this population?
| Step | Focus |
|---|---|
| **Recognize cues** | Deviation from expected for age: dyspnea, activity intolerance; equipment (in-and-out catheter, insulin pump, nebulizer, service animal); disability cues (wheelchair, white cane, extended test time) `[Engage]` |
| **Analyze cues** | Likely cause: service animal → mental health or seizure disorder; activity intolerance → heart or respiratory; braces/walker → neuromuscular/MSK `[Engage]` |
| **Prioritize hypotheses** | ==What leads to readmission==: knowledge deficit, lack of resources, nonadherence |
| **Generate solutions** | Coordinate services; connect to disciplines and community (nutrition, specialists, OT, interpreters, support groups) |
| **Take actions** | Assessments, VS, labs, reinforce test teaching, give and evaluate meds; give a nonverbal child a voice; teach devices, resources, sibling needs, family self-care |
| **Evaluate outcomes** | Effective? Adjust. ==Rely heavily on parent report==; judge holistically: coping, social, academic, physical |

> [!example] Worked example — Amanda, 12 `[Engage case study]`

## Self-quiz

> [!question]- 1. Which is a chronic illness but not a disability: asthma, lupus, or vision loss?
> Asthma. Lupus and vision loss can be both.

> [!question]- 2. How should a nurse limit harm from painful procedures in a preterm infant?
> Do only necessary procedures; provide skin-to-skin or maternal breast milk during them.

> [!question]- 3. A 10-mo-old doesn't respond to her name or point at objects. Interpretation and action?
> Possible early ASD signs; refer for developmental evaluation and early intervention.

> [!question]- 4. When are standardized developmental assessments done per the lecture?
> 12, 18, and 30 mo (Engage summary and AAP: 9, 18, 30 mo; Engage adds 4 yr).

> [!question]- 5. A toddler on home oxygen keeps pulling the cannula; mom is exhausted. Two nursing priorities?
> Assess parental coping/sleep and offer mindfulness, support groups, counseling, targeted education; keep supporting toddler autonomy with limited choices.

> [!question]- 6. Who delivers augmented-language intervention, and on what schedule?
> Speech therapist, twice weekly for 12 weeks, plus parent home exercises.

> [!question]- 7. Parent of a 4-yr-old with diabetes seems flat and says she can't keep up. Action?
> Screen her for depression and stress; refer to resources.

> [!question]- 8. A 4-yr-old with a mild disability: inclusive or special-ed preschool?
> Inclusive: greater language and social gains, no harm to typical peers.

> [!question]- 9. A 9-yr-old with a chronic illness misses school every 2 weeks. Nursing actions?
> Attendance plan with the school, appointments outside school hours, advocate for IEP/assistive tech/remote work/home-bound instruction, psychosocial support.

> [!question]- 10. A 15-yr-old with diabetes skips glucose checks to fit in. Best approach?
> Expected autonomy struggle: assess executive function, stress self-management and adherence, encourage family check-ins with parents handling refills/appointments, talk to the teen directly.

> [!question]- 11. Teaching topics for a 16-yr-old with CP who uses a wheelchair?
> Puberty, healthy relationships, consent, inappropriate touching and how to report it, contraception, online safety, at her cognitive level. Her sexual violence risk is 3×.

> [!question]- 12. An immobile 17-yr-old starts hormonal menstrual suppression. Key safety concern?
> Thrombus risk, increased with immobility.

> [!question]- 13. NAPC steps, and why no charting during it?
> Normalize, Ask, Pause, Connect. Not charting shows active listening; chart afterward.

> [!question]- 14. Where should the nurse ask a 13-yr-old about mood?
> Privately, without the parent present; talk with the parent separately too.

> [!question]- 15. Siblings aged 2, 7, and 15: who is most at risk for adverse responses?
> The 7-yr-old (peak 4–11 yr).

> [!question]- 16. A 6-yr-old sibling wants to visit the PICU. Response?
> Usually better to visit with support; refer to child life to prepare and debrief.

> [!question]- 17. Parent says, "I just try not to think about it." Coping type, risk, and help?
> Avoidant coping (psychological inflexibility): brief relief, then anxiety/depression. Offer ACT, mindfulness, support network.

> [!question]- 18. Car-travel teaching for a child with a trach and a ventilator?
> Trained passenger who can manage dislodgement, adjust seat angle, equipment below the window line, 2× power + charged backup + vehicle connection, CPST referral.

> [!question]- 19. Who's more likely bullied: a child with intellectual disability or a wheelchair user? Why?
> Intellectual disability: not apparent, so behavior is read as defiance or laziness.

> [!question]- 20. Highly stressed parent; child has bruises in different stages with a vague story. Priority?
> Suspect non-accidental trauma; report as a mandated reporter. Safety overrides confidentiality.

> [!question]- 21. Best evidence home suction teaching worked?
> Caregiver demonstrates suctioning and explains it back (teach-back).

> [!question]- 22. Most impactful goal for children with chronic conditions, and how?
> Preventing readmission, through thorough family training and acute–home care partnerships.

> [!question]- 23. Name the ICF "F-words."
> Function, family, fun, friends, future.
